## Chair

**Vote:** All three lenses vote fund-if, with low-to-mid conviction (moat 3, offer 4, product 5). Nobody votes to fund outright or to kill.
**Biggest shared risk:** Crediflow (Manchester, seed-funded) launched a UK memo generator in Oct 2026 that fills the lender's own template with source traceability. All three lenses say "cited and human-approved" is not yet a reason to switch.
**Sharpest disagreement:** Product calls the kill criteria concrete. Offer says a price band named after a free demo measures curiosity. Moat says the criteria miss the real killer and wants a dated Crediflow/no-funder kill line.
**Strongest version:** A fixed-fee, service-first check that credit files are ready for audit. It is for non-bank SME lenders whose warehouse funders or guarantee schemes check where figures came from, and it works in the lender's own template. It is not a SaaS memo tool for lenders and brokers.

## Shared risks

Raised by all three lenses (moat, offer, product):

1. **Crediflow already makes the same claim, in the UK, with named customers.** It has a spreading tool (Jun 2026) and a memo generator (Oct 2026) that fills the lender's own template with source traceability. Its named customers are AddWorking Capital and Iveco Capital, and it reportedly raised a £1.5m seed. Moat: "slightly better loses to the incumbent", and the method can be copied (Crediflow has already copied it). Offer: a buyer comparing the two gets the same result with more effort and more supplier risk (one employed individual). Product: the edge exists only if traceability is a felt pain *and* Crediflow doesn't already meet it. The bet does not yet say how it differs.
2. **Two buyers in one bet, and the broker may have nothing to replace.** *Who* names both non-bank lender analysts and NACFB brokers. The bet itself says brokers often forward raw statements and that a broker pack is "a sales document". Lenders may re-spread whatever brokers send. Research found no source either way. The ~1,400 figure is the NACFB membership count (1,414 firms), not firms seen spreading by hand. All three lenses ask the owner to pick one buyer (D2, D10).
3. **Lenders' existing data stack may already handle spreading, leaving only the part analysts won't hand over.** Codat, Equifax Share by Bank, Validis, Credit Kudos and nCino already sit in lender stacks. Moat: they control the channel and can add memo drafting cheaply (Plaid shipped LendScore 2 on 6 Oct 2026). Offer: for many lenders there is nothing left to switch from. Product: the product shrinks to a citation layer over someone else's data, applied to the memo's judgement, "the part they least want to hand over" (D3).
4. **The owner can't legally run the test yet.** All three lenses make D7 a precondition. The employment contract has an IP clause, the employer works in merchant-funding risk, and non-solicit and conflict-of-interest terms may apply. There is no data-protection basis for running real statements. Until this is settled, no paid offer, no pilot on real data, and possibly no outreach to the owner's own network.
5. **There is no price, no unit and no figure for hours saved.** No memo vendor publishes a price. The only visible prices are data-layer ones (Codat £25 per company per month, Equifax £2.82–£3.87 per connection), and offer warns a memo offer may be anchored down to those. No UK figure exists for analyst hours per deal; the only one found ("30–40% of time on memos") is US vendor marketing. All three lenses send this to evidence and numbers.

Raised by two lenses:

6. **The problem statement is an assumption, and the buyer's loudest pain is elsewhere** (offer, product; moat via D4/D11). No UK analyst or broker has been quoted saying spreading takes an afternoon or that compliance asks where figures came from. Brokers' top frustrations on record are lenders changing their minds (56%) and stricter underwriting (34%). Much limited-company lending sits outside the FCA, so the traceability pull, if it exists, comes from funders, guarantee schemes or credit committees. That changes the buyer and the pitch.
7. **The success metric measures curiosity, not commitment** (offer, moat). "Agree to a 30-minute look" and a price band named after a free demo cost the person nothing. The 3-of-20 threshold has no baseline: the BridgeTrade 2026 reply rate hasn't been pulled. Kill criterion 2 has no date. The 2-week appetite doesn't match a 6-week window (D6).

Raised by one lens:

8. **Nothing builds up to stop a rival** (moat). There is no data asset (statements belong to the client), no switching cost and no licence. Distribution is one person messaging 20 contacts, which doesn't get cheaper with scale. Kaaj already links policy exceptions to past precedent.
9. **No named user** (product). The bet names a role, not a person, and the owner can't act as the user. There is no real analyst whose last memo the demo would rebuild.
10. **A guarantee the evidence can't support** (offer). Extraction accuracy can't be guaranteed (see the 2019 test that found duplicated Ocrolus transactions). Only a citation-coverage guarantee is honest, and it must hold on 3 sample statements before it is offered.

## Where the board disagrees

- **How much is here.** Product scores 5 because the bet aims at a finished job (the memo) rather than a data dump, has real non-goals, and can ship whole in weeks. Moat scores 3 because nothing in it builds up: the method is copyable and has already been copied. Offer sits between them at 4: the claim can be checked, which makes it believable, but buyers don't yet value it. The board agrees the work is well shaped. It disagrees on whether a well-shaped product here is worth anything to a buyer.
- **Whether the kill criteria work.** Product calls them concrete, with a number and a date. Offer says they measure curiosity: a free look and a price band cost the buyer nothing. Moat says they test the wrong thing and wants a dated Crediflow/no-funder kill criterion. The board does not agree whether the current test can kill the bet.
- **Whether "cited" is an edge.** Offer sees a fairly open certainty angle, backed by Freeths commentary on senior-manager accountability, that can carry an honest guarantee. Moat sees a feature Crediflow already ships: "slightly better loses." Product makes it conditional on the pain being felt unprompted. These lead to different next steps. Offer would sharpen the guarantee; moat would look for a different angle or asset.
- **Whether the broker side is worth exploring.** Moat floats brokers as a possibly unserved corner, packaging deals before they reach a lender, with NACFB as a one-to-many channel. Product thinks a broker memo solves half a problem if lenders re-spread anyway. Offer says the broker may have no spreading step to replace. Moat sees the broker side as an opening; product and offer see a dead end.
- **Whether funders are a channel or just an interview question.** Moat treats funder or guarantee-scheme pressure as a possible secret and distribution route: one funder points several lenders at the tool. Offer and product treat D4 as something to ask about, not something to build the pitch on.
- **Whether low market prices help or hurt.** Offer holds both views. Low data-layer prices mean a one-off assessment doesn't compete with a subscription line, but they may also anchor a memo price down to £3–£25. The board hasn't settled which applies, and only the numbers step can.

## The strongest version

The pitched bet is a memo tool for lenders *and* brokers, competing on "cited and human-approved", and the board finds it weak on that ground: Crediflow has the same claim, a template fill and customers. The strongest business the board can see from these memos differs in four ways.

- **One buyer: non-bank SME lenders with outside funders.** These are 10–50 person lenders funded by warehouse lines or using British Business Bank guarantee schemes, where someone outside the credit team checks the files. If the pull for traceability exists, it comes from there and not from the FCA (D4, D11). That makes the funder a possible route to several lenders at once, which is moat's only candidate for a sales channel that scales.
- **Sell an outcome, not a tool: a fixed-fee assessment that credit files are ready for audit.** The owner takes a lender's recent memos and rebuilds or checks them so that every figure traces to a source transaction, in the lender's own template, with the analyst signing off. It is sold as an expert service, and the software is the owner's own instrument. This matches the project's real goal of freelancing as a credible expert in 6 months. It also avoids a software fight with a funded incumbent and matches the bet's own non-goal of "no SaaS before a paid pilot".
- **Treat Crediflow and the lender's LLM drafts as inputs to check, not rivals.** As lenders trial AI drafting (Crediflow's or their own), the gap grows for an independent second-line check that every figure is backed by a source. Positioned that way, the stack vendors moving up (moat's second killer) create demand rather than absorb it.
- **Build what the service keeps, from the first engagement.** Each lender's policy rules, exception precedents and template map become the asset that makes the 10th assessment faster and better than the 1st. That answers moat's question about what builds up.

This version depends on one fact nobody has yet: whether a funder, guarantee scheme or credit committee has actually asked where a figure in a credit file came from. If the 5 interviews and the research step turn up no such finding, this version collapses too, and the honest result is to kill or re-scope. It also depends on the employment contract. If D7 rules out paid offers before the owner goes freelance, the next 6 weeks should be framed as problem interviews only, with the paid assessment launched once the owner has left.

## What is strong
- **The claim can be checked.** "Every figure is cited and human-approved" (bet title) is something a buyer can verify on their own statement in minutes. That makes it a believable promise and a basis for an honest guarantee (see Conditions).
- **The certainty angle is fairly open.** Research ('Gap I see') found no competitor page, apart from Crediflow's reported source traceability, that claims every figure is cited and human-approved. The Freeths 2026 commentary says senior managers carry accountability for hallucinated figures in credit assessments. That gives the certainty claim something real to stand on, though it is law-firm commentary and not a buyer's words.
- **The test is cheap and the first offer is concrete.** The appetite is 2 weeks and $20. The bet already names a fixed-fee assessment or pilot as the first thing to sell ('Expected outcome'), which is the right shape for a first offer: small, bounded, and sold before any SaaS (non_goals).
- **Visible prices are low, which helps an assessment offer.** The only list prices found are Codat at £25 per active company per month and Equifax at £3.87 to £2.82 per connection ('Prices'). A one-off fixed-fee assessment does not have to compete with a subscription line item.

## What would kill it
1. **The value isn't the buyer's value.** The problem statement is marked "ASSUMPTION, not yet quoted from anyone" (bet, 'Problem in their words'). Research found no UK analyst or broker quote about spreading time or compliance asking where a figure came from ('Not found'). Brokers' loudest complaints are lenders changing their minds (56%) and stricter underwriting (34%), not spreading. If traceability isn't the pain the buyer names first, the offer has no first sentence. *Test:* in the D1 'last memo you wrote' interviews, count how many of 5 raise time, errors or traceability before any demo. Fewer than 2 means the headline is wrong.
2. **Crediflow already makes the same offer, with less effort for the buyer and more certainty about the supplier.** Crediflow (Manchester, reported £1.5m seed) launched a memo generator in Oct 2026 that fills *the lender's own templates* with source traceability, and it has named customers (AddWorking Capital, Iveco Capital) (research, 'Direct competitors'). This bet doesn't say it fills the lender's template. Its supplier is one person who is still employed (D7). A buyer comparing the two gets the same outcome with more effort and more supplier risk. *Test:* ask 3 heads of credit whether they have seen Crediflow, and get one line on why they would choose this instead. If nobody can give that line, there is no reason to switch.
3. **Switching costs more than the gain.** To switch, a lender has to send customer statements to an unvetted individual, which means data-protection terms and vendor due diligence (D7). Many may already get categorised transactions from Codat, Equifax, Validis or their loan-origination system (D3, research 'Indirect'). That leaves only the memo, which is the analyst's judgement and the part they least want to hand over. Brokers may not spread at all (D2), so for them there is nothing to switch from. *Test:* ask a stack question in every interview: "What would you need before you sent us one anonymised statement?" Count how many say yes without a procurement process.
- Also a risk: the only prices buyers can see are data-layer prices of £2.82 to £25 per company. A memo offer may be anchored down to that level unless its value is counted in analyst hours or audit findings. Nobody has that number yet (research: "no UK figure found for hours per deal").
